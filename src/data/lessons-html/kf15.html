0:08 MINDFULNESS & MEDITATION TRAINING

In this lesson we begin looking at KF Confession 4:

We judged ourselves and others with contempt and felt justified in our wrath.

In KF, we teach mindfulness exercises that we ourselves have found helpful, but there are many other options out there that you might try. When life’s experiments trigger judgment and wrath, you need to be skilled in mindfulness, so take time to train, if not every day, then regularly. See if you can work up to spending twenty minutes, twice each day, in mindfulness practice and meditation. If you are raising young children, you might want to take small breaks throughout the day to explore mindfulness together. You can invite your children to join you in short times of practice. Teach them to simply notice their breath for one minute, and then see what happens.

The Mercy Meditation supports our efforts to reorient the misdirected energy described in KF Confession 4.[1] With the addition of this meditation, you will have four different practices available to you. Explore the same meditation for a whole month to give you time to discover whether it works for you. Or you might practice each of the four meditations for five minutes, giving you twenty minutes of meditation practice.

By practicing mindfulness, you bring your mind and emotions under the guidance of your own will. You are then free to connect to the Holy Spirit in a moment. Undisciplined minds are like wayward and unruly puppies jumping at every opportunity to impose their own primitive wills. Puppies are big enough to do damage and, like all beginners, are in desperate need of training. Your mind and emotions also need training, just like those puppies. Be kind to yourself during this process, and remember that there are seasons of effort and seasons of rest.

The Mercy Meditation

Assume a meditation posture, and breathe. You might wish to take some notes in your KF journal. (Pause) Draw your mind away from the events of the day, from the past or the future, and from others. Focus on what is happening inside your skin. Settle into being present and aware in this moment. (Pause for 30 seconds)

And now, reflect for thirty seconds on each of the following three questions. Observe your feelings and thoughts without judgment, and notice your body’s reactions.

In what areas am I most critical of myself? (Repeat) (Pause for 30 seconds)

Pause, and if you haven’t already done so, take thirty seconds to jot down your thoughts. (Pause for 30 seconds)

For what do I need to forgive myself? (Repeat) (Pause for 30 seconds)

Pause. You might make a note of what came to mind. (Pause for 30 seconds)

How do I treat myself when I make a mistake? (Repeat) (Pause for 30 seconds)

Pause, and if you wish, write for thirty seconds. (Pause for 30 seconds)

Now experiment with bringing compassion to yourself in some way. For example, call to mind someone who loves you just as you are. This might be someone divine, like God or Jesus, or perhaps someone mortal and human. You might call to mind one of God’s other creatures. (Pause) Imagine seeing yourself through their eyes. You might give yourself an appreciative hug. (Pause) However you choose to bring compassion to yourself, just observe and rest for one minute. Practice letting go of judgment and breathe. (Pause for 1 minute)

And pause. In this practice, we plant seeds of mercy—not only for ourselves but for others. Mercy is the civil law of God’s kingdom.[2]

Our hymn for this lesson is When I Survey the Wondrous Cross. This is one of 750 hymns written by Isaac Watts, many of which we still sing today. Isaac Watts was an evangelical non-conformist Christian in seventeenth century England. Both he and his father were excluded from the established church and from any government position, and Isaac’s father spent years in prison for his Christian convictions.

Let’s listen to our hymn.

When I survey the wondrous cross

On which the Prince of Glory died,

My richest gain I count but loss,

And pour contempt on all my pride.[3]

Take a deep breath.

0:20 KARIS CONFESSION

0:21 TOOLBOX

Hopefully, you have a Shoulds Worksheet in front of you as we explore KF Confession 4 together. This Confession reminds us that when we are misoriented, we judge ourselves and others with contempt and feel justified in our wrath. Our judgments are the main obstacles to our growth and development, and those judgments inevitably undermine the growth of others.

Paul’s letter to the Galatians reminds us that while striving to become like Jesus, we can experience intense self-hate because we have inadvertently adopted an additional set of standards by which we measure and mercilessly judge ourselves. Our KF tools help us to courageously endure moments of self-hate while we grow.

As you give up your search for the Glory of Me!, you will look back with compassion on your former life of striving, and you will experience increasing gratitude to God for His patience and love in supporting you while you find your way home.

Shoulds and taboos are the artificial internal motivators of the Idol. They are the legal systems, or law, that we use to motivate ourselves and to impose our will on others. Externalized shoulds express themselves in two different ways: we either try to force others to conform to our standards, or we believe that others are forcing their standards on us. These standards that we think others are imposing on us are often standards that we are imposing on ourselves. When others do not fulfill our shoulds, we feel toward them all the fury that we unconsciously feel toward ourselves; and when others appear to impose their shoulds on us, we feel compelled to comply with them, resist them, or fight against them. Externalized shoulds explain why we need to impose our will on others and, also, why we feel so much judgment from others. We assume that others are judging us with the same wrath with which we judge ourselves. God lovingly gave us the Ten Commandments, but we have imposed upon ourselves ten gazillion other commandments in the form of claims and shoulds.

Let’s look at Part Two of our Shoulds Worksheet. One KF member observed that we are not looking deeply for spiritual insight here. We are looking deeply at how crazy we are!

Part Two: Looking Deeply

Recognize the “mistake”

Ask yourself: What did I do that was “wrong” or has caused my discomfort? Here are some examples of how you might answer this question: I said this thing and another person reacted negatively. I messed up my interview and lost that job opportunity. I allowed that organization to impose their ideas on me. I wasn’t strong enough to follow through. I can’t make any friends.

Write down your shoulds

Should-statements often begin with should-phrases, such as I should always, I should never, I should be able to, I should feel, or I should know. On this worksheet Nancy has given you a number of examples of how you might finish these phrases. Here is how a KF member finished one of his should-phrases: “I am committed to the idea that I should always be able to manage my limitations so that nothing can interfere with any of my plans.”

Define perfection according to your Idol

When you write this statement, you can have a little fun describing the extremes of perfection to which you aspire. Sometimes it’s helpful to see how crazy our dreams of glory can be, and smile.

Identify your limitations

In this step, we deal with the real limitations of being material creatures living in a misoriented world. You might also think about the limitations that God’s logos imposes on you personally—limitations like honesty, purity, honor, and trustworthiness. You cannot expect outcomes that are productive and healthy if you lie, practice addictive behaviors, dishonor your own or another’s body, and live chaotically—disrespecting God, others, and yourself. So, what are your limitations?

Notice when you are using the words should and shouldn’t in your thinking and conversation. Those words can reveal the thou shalts and thou shalt nots­ that you have created to support your Idol Self. You might try this trick: When you notice that you are using the word should, try replacing it with the word could or might. (Repeat) See what happens. Nancy learned that trick from her husband a long time ago, and it has helped her recognize her shoulds. You might try it for yourself.

Our prayer could go something like this:

Merciful God, help me let go of my shoulds. (Repeat)

Take a deep breath.

1:00 BIBLE TEACHING

This is KF Confession 4:

We judged ourselves and others with contempt and felt justified in our wrath.

Let’s turn to the Gospel of Luke, chapter 6.

Perhaps by now you have scheduled a regular time to practice your KF tools. During that time, you might explore a Shoulds Worksheet. To help you identify your own particular shoulds, review chapter 3 of Neurosis and Human Growth and the chapters that describe your Idol.[4] You might make a running list of the shoulds that you have discovered in your own life and then see how that list changes during your KF training. Notice that Nancy is not saying that you should do any of these things. She is just reminding you of a few things that you might do during your KF time to help you achieve the oriented outcomes that you desire. In the months ahead, you can review all of these tools, especially when extra training is offered. We hope that using the tools will become part of your soul-care routine. You are a creature in time and have all the time you need to grow. How does that feel? You have all the time you need to grow. That feels pretty good, doesn’t it?

Here is Nancy’s translation of Luke 6:41–42, a passage that is probably quite familiar to many of you:

41Why do you regard the twig that is in your sibling's eye, but do not fully observe the beam that is in your own eye? 42Or how can you say to your sibling, “Sibling, allow me to take out the twig that is in your eye,” when you yourself do not see the beam that is in your own eye? You pretender! First take the beam out of your own eye, and then you will see straight in order to take out the twig that is in your sibling's eye.

Take a deep breath.

Whenever we want others to do things our way, we have already judged that their way is wrong based on our righteous and omniscient wisdom. Our judgment is often the externalization of unachieved standards that we have imposed on ourselves. We desperately want others to fulfill those standards for us because we imagine that their compliance will stop our self-hate; we hope that changing them will take us effortlessly into our imagined glory. That’s how we end up in other people’s business.

This metaphor of twigs and beams is about the relative importance of our business and another person’s business, and it reflects the problem of our misoriented perspective. When we impose our legal systems on other people, our perspective becomes distorted, and we start focusing on their business rather than taking care of our own. Instead of changing ourselves, we try to fix or change them. In this metaphor, Jesus uses the Greek word for a construction beam—something that we build with. These beams support the temple of our Idol Self, but this temple is so vulnerable that we need others to validate it by accommodating our pride system. If they comply with our shoulds, they must expend on us the resources they have been given for dealing with their own beams. However, most people will not participate in our search for glory. They see things differently. What in our eyes has the importance of a structural beam holding up our temple is a mere twig in someone else’s eyes. Other people cannot possibly understand how important our beam is to us.

What we don’t understand and refuse to explore is how our problems with other people are mercifully exposing the tyranny of our shoulds and self-hate. We try to avoid that humbling exposure by employing the same forceful, manipulative tactics that we have always used to make others support our Idol’s pride. We might get compliance from others in the moment, but we damage the long-term possibilities for relationship. When we impose our shoulds on someone else, we avoid the painful process of removing the beam in our own eye, which means actively demolishing our Idol’s temple. Those twigs that we see in another’s eye are often our own beams reflected from afar. It is a problem of perspective and interpretation. We see in another what is actually within ourselves.

Your feelings of discomfort and your fear of self-hate are used by the Idol to justify your clumsy efforts to remove the twig in someone else’s eye. No wonder people don’t trust you! Have you noticed that when you try to fix other people, whether actively or in your own mind, the focus goes outside your own skin and into someone else’s business? This is why getting into other people’s business is so dangerous and even rather stupid; you are not motivated by the truth about the situation but driven by the compelling need to keep the beams of your Idol’s temple securely in place.

If you want to be real and express the love of God, you must give up imposing your shoulds on others and on yourself. Go back to the list Love or Pride? Is your attitude and your behavior in line with love?

Once you know your own business, you can ignore everything else. Isn’t that amazing? You don’t even have to think about it. The problem is that we often want to think about everything else and everyone else in order to avoid our own business. Most of us have spent our whole lives avoiding our business. You may be asking right now: What is my business?[5] Your trustworthiness and your healing are your business. Your fear is also your business—and so is your true glory.

Our prayer might go something like this:

Lord, help me stick to my business. (Repeat)

Take a deep breath.

1:10 INTEGRATION

Sip some purified water and prepare to take some notes. Assume a meditation posture, and breathe. (Pause for 10 seconds)

Here is our integration focus: Consider a twig in someone else’s eye, something that you would like to fix. (Repeat) (Pause for 30 seconds)

The FAITH Process

Feelings

As you consider a twig in someone else’s eye, something that you would like to fix, how does it feel? (Pause) Your feelings are yours to teach you about yourself, to guide you, and to train you. They can often be uncomfortable. (Pause) You can learn to recognize and honor your feelings without allowing them to master you. Recognize with compassion whatever you may be experiencing, and breathe. (Pause for 30 seconds)

Awareness without judgment

Allow whatever you are experiencing to rest in your awareness. Breathe, and hold your experience up to the light as you consider a twig in someone else’s eye. (Pause) Feelings and passing thoughts are not right or wrong; they just are. Give yourself permission now to explore them without judgment, and breathe. (Pause for 30 seconds)

Inquiry: What is my body saying?

Breathe, and bring your attention to your body. Notice what it is doing when you consider that twig in someone else’s eye. What sensations are there? (Pause) For example, you might recognize what frustration or vindictive triumph feels like in your body, or perhaps what anxiety or hurt pride feels like. (Pause) If your sensations are uncomfortable, you can move your attention to an area of your body that is not reacting, and rest. When you are ready, return to respectfully observe those uncomfortable sensations. Breathe. (Pause for 30 seconds)

Talk to Jesus

You might imagine that you are leaning back in the safe and loving arms of Jesus as together you consider a twig in someone else’s eye. Isn’t it wonderful that He will consider that twig with you? (Pause) Have you judged the twig as good or evil? What are your reasons? What story do you want to tell? (Pause) Is this judgment a regular pattern in your life? (Pause) Are you afraid of something—perhaps of others or of the loss of your hope for glory? Are you avoiding your own business or your True Self? (Pause) Observe your responses as just information, and breathe with a long exhaling breath. (Pause for 30 seconds)

Heal

God created you to have all of these feelings, and for thousands of years human beings have been feeling like you feel when they have considered fixing someone else. You are not alone in your feelings. You are a human creature. (Pause) Feelings are just chemical reactions in your body bringing you information. With training, you can choose what you will do with those feelings. (Pause) You can observe and learn something from what you experience. Breathe with compassion for yourself and for others who long to fix another person. Help your body process with a long exhaling breath. (Pause for 30 seconds)

(Optional) Continue to breathe while I test for further integration.

Take a deep breath.
